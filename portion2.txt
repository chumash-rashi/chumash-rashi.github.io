
==================================================
SECOND PORTION — Vayikra (Leviticus), Chapters 6-7
==================================================

Now the first pasuk of the Second Portion.

Verse 12: God spoke to Moses, saying.

Again an introductory phrase — a new instruction is coming.

Now the next pasuk.

Verse 13: "This is the offering of Aaron and his descendants that they must offer up to God, beginning on the day when one of them is anointed as high priest: one-tenth of an ephah of fine wheat flour as a perpetual grain-offering, half in the morning and half in the afternoon."

This pasuk introduces a special ongoing grain-offering belonging to Aaron and the priests. Every high priest, starting from the day he is anointed, must bring this offering every single day — a tenth of an ephah of fine flour, split in half, half in the morning and half in the afternoon.

Now the Rashi on this pasuk.

Rashi is bothered by the phrase "this is the offering of Aaron and his descendants." Does every priest bring this daily offering, or only the high priest?

Rashi answers: even ordinary priests bring a tenth of an ephah on the day they are first initiated into the priestly service. But the high priest brings it every day throughout his service — because the verse says "a perpetual grain-offering." The ongoing daily requirement applies only to the high priest.

Now the next pasuk.

Verse 14: "It must be made — that is, fried — in oil in a shallow frying pan; it must be brought to the frying pan after being scalded in water and baked in an oven. The grain-offering must be broken into pieces. You must offer it up with the intention to please God."

This pasuk describes the complex preparation of the daily priestly grain-offering. It involves several steps: scalding in boiling water, baking in an oven, frying in a pan, and then breaking into pieces.

Now the first Rashi on this pasuk.

Rashi is bothered by the word "murbechet" — scalded. What exactly does this mean?

Rashi answers: it means the dough is scalded in boiling hot water, as much as necessary. It is a thorough scalding process.

Now the second Rashi.

Rashi is bothered by the word "tufine" — baked. What does this word mean?

Rashi answers: it means "baked many times." The process is: first it is scalded, then baked in an oven, and then fried in a pan. Multiple rounds of cooking.

Now the third Rashi.

Rashi is bothered by the phrase "a grain-offering of pieces." How exactly is it broken into pieces — is it crumbled completely?

Rashi answers: no — it is not crumbled into small fragments, because then there would be nothing to take a fistful from. Rather, each loaf is folded in half and then into fourths, both vertically and horizontally. The pieces are not fully separated — they are folded, not crumbled — and then burned on the Altar together.

Now the next pasuk.

Verse 15: "The priest from among his sons who is anointed in his stead must prepare it; it is an eternal allotment given to God. All of it must be burned up."

If the high priest is succeeded by one of his sons, that son takes over this daily offering. And crucially — this priestly grain-offering is entirely burned. None of it is eaten.

Now the first Rashi on this pasuk.

Rashi is bothered by the phrase "the anointed one among his sons." What does this mean exactly?

Rashi answers: it simply means the anointed son who succeeds him as high priest.

Now the second Rashi.

Rashi is bothered by the phrase "all of it must be burned up" — kalil. How is this different from the same word used in the next verse?

Rashi explains: here, the grain-offering is burned entirely including what would normally be the "remainder" — no fistful is separated for eating because the whole thing goes up. In the next verse, "wholly burned up" means something slightly different: no fistful is taken from it at all, and the entire thing is offered to God equally. Both uses of "kalil" teach their own distinct nuance about how fully priestly offerings are consumed.

Now the next pasuk.

Verse 16: "Every grain-offering brought by a priest on his own behalf must be wholly burned up. It may not be eaten."

This is the general rule: any grain-offering a priest brings personally — whether obligatory or voluntary — is entirely consumed on the Altar. Priests do not eat their own grain-offerings.

Now the Rashi on this pasuk.

Rashi is bothered by the word "kalil" — wholly. What specific nuance does this word carry here?

Rashi answers: "kalil" here means all of it is offered up to God equally. Every part has equal status — there is no distinction between a "fistful" portion and a "remainder" portion, because the whole offering belongs entirely to God.

Now the next pasuk.

Verse 17: God spoke to Moses, saying.

A new section is beginning.

Now the next pasuk.

Verse 18: "Speak to Aaron and to his sons, saying: This is the regulation of the sin-offering. The sin-offering must be slaughtered before God in the place where the ascent-offering is slaughtered. It is a sacrifice of superior holiness."

Now the Torah moves to the chatat — the sin-offering. The sin-offering must be slaughtered in the same place as the olah — in the north of the Courtyard. And like the olah, it carries the status of kodesh kodashim, a sacrifice of superior holiness.

Now the next pasuk.

Verse 19: "Any priest who is fit to offer it up as a sin-offering may eat it. It must be eaten in a holy place, in the Courtyard of the Tent of Meeting."

The sin-offering belongs to the priests — they eat it. But only a priest who is fit to perform the service of this specific offering may receive a portion.

Now the first Rashi on this pasuk.

Rashi is bothered by the phrase "the one who offers it up as a sin-offering." What exactly does this phrase mean?

Rashi answers: the Hebrew here — "hamechatei" — means the one who carries out the acts of service that make it a sin-offering. It is a description of the qualified priest performing the service.

Now the second Rashi.

Rashi is bothered by the implication that only the specific priest who performed the service eats it. But later the verse says "every male among the priests may eat it." How do we reconcile these?

Rashi answers: the phrase "the one who offers it" is not meant to exclude other priests. Rather, it excludes a priest who was ritually defiled at the time the blood was dashed. Such a priest, being unfit to perform the service at that moment, receives no portion of the meat.

Now the next pasuk.

Verse 20: "Any food that touches its meat will become holy like it. If any of its blood spurts onto a garment, you must wash the area of the garment onto which it spurted in a holy place."

Two laws here: first, anything that absorbs flavor from the sin-offering takes on its holy status. Second, if the blood of a sin-offering splashes onto a garment, that garment must be washed inside the Courtyard.

Now the first Rashi on this pasuk.

Rashi is bothered by "whatever touches its meat" — touches it and does what?

Rashi answers: this refers to any food that comes in contact with the sin-offering meat and absorbs some of its flavor.

Now the second Rashi.

Rashi is bothered by "will become holy." What kind of holiness does this absorbed food take on?

Rashi answers: it takes on the same status as the sin-offering itself. If the sin-offering was invalid, the absorbed food becomes invalid. If it was valid, the absorbed food may be eaten — but only under the same restrictions as the sin-offering.

Now the third Rashi.

Rashi is bothered by the instruction to wash the garment "in a holy place" — inside the Courtyard. Why must the washing happen specifically there?

Rashi explains: the verse means exactly what it says — the location of the bloodstain on the garment must be washed inside the Courtyard. The washing cannot be done outside.

Now the fourth Rashi.

Rashi is bothered by the grammatical form of the word "yizeh" — that which will be sprinkled. What kind of word is this?

Rashi answers: "yizeh" means "that which will be sprinkled" — it is a future-form expression, and Rashi cites a parallel usage elsewhere in Scripture to clarify the grammatical pattern.

Now the next pasuk.

Verse 21: "Any earthenware vessel in which it has been cooked must be shattered. If it is cooked in a copper vessel, it must be purged and rinsed with water."

If a sin-offering is cooked in an earthenware vessel, that vessel must be broken afterward. If it is cooked in a metal vessel, it must be thoroughly scoured and rinsed.

Now the first Rashi on this pasuk.

Rashi is bothered by why the earthenware vessel must be shattered. Why not just wash it?

Rashi answers: because whatever has been absorbed into the earthenware vessel has immediately become leftover sacrificial meat — notar — which is forbidden. Since earthenware absorbs flavor in a way that cannot be fully released by washing, the vessel is permanently contaminated. It must be broken. This applies to any sacrifice, not only the sin-offering.

Now the second Rashi.

Rashi is bothered by the word "u-morak" — purged. What does this mean?

Rashi answers: the word is related to the cleansing lotions used by women — a thorough, intensive scouring. In Old French, it means "escourment." The metal vessel must be scoured clean.

Now the third Rashi.

Rashi is bothered by the two-step process for the copper vessel — purged AND rinsed. Why are both needed?

Rashi answers: the scouring releases the food absorbed in the walls of the vessel, and the rinsing clears away what was loosened. Earthenware can never achieve this — the absorbed flavor remains no matter how much you clean it, which is why earthenware must be broken.

Now the next pasuk.

Verse 22: "Every male among the priests may eat it; it is a sacrifice of superior holiness."

All male priests in good standing may eat from the sin-offering.

Now the Rashi on this pasuk.

Rashi is bothered by this verse in light of the earlier statement that "the one who offers it up may eat it." Was that not a restriction to just one priest?

Rashi answers: exactly — this verse clarifies the earlier one. "Every male among the priests may eat it" confirms that the earlier phrase was never meant to restrict eating to a single priest. It only excluded those unfit to perform the service — like a ritually defiled priest — from receiving a portion.

Now the next pasuk.

Verse 23: "But any sin-offering some of whose blood was mistakenly brought into the Tent of Meeting in order to effect atonement in the Sanctuary must not be eaten; it must be burned in fire."

There is a category of sin-offerings whose blood is meant to be applied inside the Sanctuary — on the inner Altar, the Parochet curtain, or the Kapporet. If the blood of an "outer" sin-offering — one whose blood should go only on the outer Altar — was accidentally brought inside, it becomes invalid and must be burned.

Now the first Rashi on this pasuk.

Rashi is bothered by the phrase "any sin-offering." What specific teaching is this conveying?

Rashi answers: this teaches that if any amount of blood from a sin-offering that should properly be applied outside the Sanctuary was brought inside, the entire offering becomes invalid and must be burned.

Now the second Rashi.

Rashi is bothered by the word "ve-chol" — any. Why add this word?

Rashi answers: "any" is an inclusive term, meant to extend this rule beyond just the sin-offering to include other sacrifices as well.

Now we move into Chapter 7.

Verse 1: "The following is the regulation of the guilt-offering: It is an offering of superior holiness."

Now the Torah turns to the asham — the guilt-offering. Like the sin-offering, it is kodesh kodashim.

Now the Rashi on this pasuk.

Rashi is bothered by the phrase "it is an offering of superior holiness." Is this just a status statement, or does it carry a specific legal implication?

Rashi answers: it means the guilt-offering is sacrificed, but a substitute animal designated for it is not itself sacrificed. This is a legal distinction from some other offerings.

Now the next pasuk.

Verse 2: "They must slaughter the guilt-offering in the place where they slaughter the ascent-offering. Its blood must be dashed upon the Altar in an encircling manner."

Like the sin-offering, the guilt-offering is slaughtered in the north of the Courtyard. Its blood is dashed on the Altar.

Now the Rashi on this pasuk.

Rashi is bothered by the plural form "they must slaughter" — rather than "he must slaughter." Why the plural?

Rashi answers: the Torah uses the plural to include the possibility of a communal guilt-offering being slaughtered by multiple participants. The plural form also connects the guilt-offering to the ascent-offering, teaching that just as a communal ascent-offering is slaughtered in the north, so too the guilt-offering.

Now the next pasuk.

Verse 3: "The priest must offer up from it all of its fat: the tail, the fat covering the innards."

This verse begins listing the parts of the guilt-offering burned on the Altar.

Now the first Rashi on this pasuk.

Rashi is bothered by why the Torah specifies these parts here. Were they not already listed for other sacrifices?

Rashi answers: the parts to be burned on the Altar were never explicitly specified for the guilt-offering before this passage, so they need to be listed here. For the sin-offering, these parts were already listed in Parashat Vayikra.

Now the second Rashi.

Rashi is bothered by the inclusion of the tail. Not all animals have a substantial tail — why mention it specifically?

Rashi answers: a guilt-offering can only be brought from rams or lambs, and regarding both of those, the fatty tail is an additional part offered on the Altar, beyond the usual fat components.

Now the next pasuk.

Verse 4: "The two kidneys, the fat on them and on the flanks. He must remove the diaphragm along with the kidneys and along with part of the liver."

Continuing the list of parts burned on the Altar.

Now the next pasuk.

Verse 5: "The priest must burn them up on the Altar as a fire-offering to God. It is a guilt-offering."

These listed parts are burned on the Altar. The offering retains its designation as a guilt-offering.

Now the Rashi on this pasuk.

Rashi is bothered by the phrase "it is a guilt-offering" stated here at the end. Why reiterate this?

Rashi answers: this is a significant legal point. The offering retains its guilt-offering status until formally re-designated. If the owner died or already achieved atonement through another animal, the animal is sent to pasture until it develops a blemish, is sold, and the proceeds go toward ascent-offerings. But if such an animal was slaughtered before being officially re-designated — even without proper intent — it cannot function as an ascent-offering. "It is a guilt-offering" means it stays a guilt-offering until properly changed.

Now the next pasuk.

Verse 6: "Every male among the priests may eat it. It must be eaten in a holy place, for it is a sacrifice of superior holiness."

All male priests may eat the guilt-offering, in the Courtyard.

Now the Rashi on this pasuk.

Rashi is bothered by the phrase "it is a sacrifice of superior holiness" — what legal teaching does this carry here?

Rashi says: this phrase is expounded with specific teachings in Torat Kohanim.

Now the next pasuk.

Verse 7: "The guilt-offering is like the sin-offering in that they share this same regulation: the portion belongs to any priest who is fit to effect atonement through it."

The guilt-offering and sin-offering share one key rule: only a priest fit to perform the service receives a portion of the meat.

Now the first Rashi on this pasuk.

Rashi is bothered by "they have one law." In what specific respect are they the same?

Rashi answers: in exactly this one following aspect — that the portion belongs to the qualified priest.

Now the second Rashi.

Rashi is bothered by the phrase "the priest who may effect atonement through it." What makes a priest fit or unfit for this?

Rashi answers: this excludes three categories: a tevul yom — someone who immersed in a mikveh but must still wait until nightfall; one who lacks atonement offerings required to fully complete his purification; and an onen — someone whose close relative died that day and who is in the initial period of mourning. None of these three may perform the service, and therefore none receives a portion.

Now the next pasuk.

Verse 8: "Any priest who is fit to offer up a person's ascent-offering, that priest may receive the hide of any ascent-offering that he has offered up."

The priest performing the service of an olah keeps its hide — the skin of the animal. The hide does not go up on the Altar; it belongs to the priest.

Now the Rashi on this pasuk.

Rashi is bothered by who receives the hide — the individual priest or all the priests?

Rashi answers: the same categories of disqualified priests — a tevul yom, one lacking atonement, and an onen — do not receive a share of the hides either.

Now the next pasuk.

Verse 9: "With regard to any grain-offering baked in an oven, or made in a deep frying pan or in a shallow frying pan, the priest who is fit to offer it up may receive it."

Any type of baked grain-offering belongs to the priests who served on that day.

Now the Rashi on this pasuk.

Rashi is bothered by a contradiction: this verse says the grain-offering goes "to the priest who offers it" — implying one priest — while another verse says it belongs to "all the sons of Aaron." How can both be true?

Rashi answers: both are true and reconciled as follows. The grain-offering goes to the priestly division — the mishmar — assigned to serve on the day the offering is brought. Not one individual priest alone, but not every priest in Israel either — specifically the group on duty that day.

Now the next pasuk.

Verse 10: "Any grain-offering, whether mixed with oil or dry, will belong to all the sons of Aaron, each individual priest like the other."

This verse covers unbaked grain-offerings, which are divided equally among all the priests.

Now the first Rashi on this pasuk.

Rashi is bothered by "mixed with oil" — which offering is this?

Rashi answers: this refers to the voluntary grain-offering that is brought with oil.

Now the second Rashi.

Rashi is bothered by "or dry" — which offering is brought dry, without oil?

Rashi answers: this refers to the grain-offering of a sinner and the grain-offering brought in cases of a suspected adulteress — both of which are brought without oil.
